Toy collections

The toy looks the same. Almost everything behind it has changed.

Australia imported US$1.42 billion of toys in 2025, and 81 cents of every dollar went to China. Beneath that one giant, the countries supplying us keep shifting — and the world's biggest toy brand has quietly changed who it makes toys for.

By Zeji Li, October 2026

1

Where the world's toys are made

Toys are one of the most concentrated trades on Earth. In 2024 China exported US$39.9 billion of toys — 64% of the world total, and more than eleven times the next country. The runner-up is more surprising: Czechia. We'll come back to why.

Australia's own suppliers tell a similar story. China has been our biggest source of toys every year since 1995. Beneath it, the order keeps changing: the United States slid from second to fifth, while Vietnam appeared from almost nowhere to take second place.

Over thirty years the market grew about seven times, from US$199 million to US$1.42 billion. China's share climbed with it, peaking at 85% in 2018. Then it started to slip, even as Australians kept buying more.

2

How they reach Australia

Draw every shipment as a line and almost all of them arrive from one direction. The 18 countries that each sent Australia more than US$2 million of toys in 2025 account for 98.7% of the total.

Take China out of the picture, and the rest of the supply is anything but stable. Two European countries, Denmark and Czechia, grew together through the early 2010s — then fell away at almost the same moment, just as Vietnam surged. When two countries rise and fall in lockstep, the cause is rarely the countries themselves.

3

The factory behind the flag

The link is LEGO. The Danish company runs six factories, with a seventh being built in Virginia, and their locations line up with the puzzle from the first map. Czechia hosts a LEGO plant at Kladno; Hungary has another at Nyíregyháza. Denmark, where LEGO was invented, now supplies Australia with just US$1.6 million of toys a year.

What LEGO makes has changed as much as where it makes it. Through the 1970s, 80s and 90s, plain brick sets were a steady part of the range. Today they have almost disappeared, while sets built around someone else's film, game or franchise have grown to a third of everything released.

4

Winners, losers, and one company

Compare Australia's toy imports in the late 1990s with the early 2020s and a clear pattern appears. Only five countries increased their share. Nineteen lost ground, led by the United States, Hong Kong and Denmark.

Zoom in on only the seven countries where LEGO manufactures, and the pattern sharpens. Denmark's share of Australia's toy imports fell from 3.3% to 0.35%. Vietnam's rose from 0.15% to 6.8%. Czechia and Hungary each grew roughly tenfold.

Denmark didn't stop making LEGO. It stopped making it in Denmark.

This chart uses a log scale: each gridline is ten times the one below, so very small and very large shares can sit on the same chart. China rises too, but LEGO is only a small part of China's much larger toy industry.

5

Who is it really for?

The sets themselves have grown. The typical LEGO set of the 1980s had 35 pieces. In the 2020s it has 158, and the biggest now runs to more than 11,000.

Bigger sets have brought older buyers. Pre-school sets are aimed at two-year-olds, while technical and model-making sets sit around nine and stretch all the way to adults. In the whole of the 2010s, LEGO released one set rated 18+. In the 2020s so far, it has released 385.

More of those sets also borrow their story. Star Wars became LEGO's first licensed theme in 1999, with 13 sets. By the 2020s, a third of everything LEGO released carried someone else's brand.

A child in 1985 might have unwrapped a 35-piece box made in Denmark. An adult in 2025 is more likely to buy a 158-piece set tied to a film franchise, made in China or Vietnam. The toy looks much the same. Almost everything behind it has changed.

Data sources

  • Toy trade: UN Comtrade, accessed via World Integrated Trade Solution (WITS), World Bank. HS 1988/92 heading 9503 (toys). Australia's imports by partner, 1995–2025; world exports by reporter, 2024. wits.worldbank.org

  • LEGO factory locations: The LEGO Group, Locations. lego.com. Coordinates from Google Maps.
